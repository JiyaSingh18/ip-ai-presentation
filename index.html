Context
Why does IP matter for AI?

AI systems touch every major IP category simultaneously — creating legal uncertainty at each layer.

The innovation stack

  • Training data — millions of copyrighted images, text, code (Copyright)
  • Algorithms & architecture — may be patented or trade secret (Patent / Trade Secret)
  • Model weights — often protected as trade secrets (Trade Secret)
  • Product name & brand — ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot (Trademark)
  • Generated output — images, text, code — who owns it? (Copyright — our focus)

Central question

When an AI model produces a poem, image, or code snippet — who is the author? Can it be copyrighted at all?

Our thesis: Copyright law was designed for human creators. AI exposes a fundamental gap — and courts are now defining the boundaries case by case.

Global AI market projected >$1.8T by 2030 — IP clarity is essential for investment and innovation.

Concept Clarity — Copyright
Copyright: legal elements & requirements

Three requirements for copyright

  1. Originality — work must originate from the author, not copied (low threshold — "sweat of brow" in India)
  2. Fixation — expressed in a tangible/perceptible form (file, recording, print)
  3. Human authorship — author must be a natural person (key AI debate)

India: Copyright Act 1957, §2(d) — "author" means the person who causes the work to be created. §13 — literary, artistic, musical, cinematographic works.

What copyright does NOT protect

  • Ideas, facts, methods — only expression is protected
  • AI-generated content with no human input — US Copyright Office 2023 guidance
  • Works in public domain — expired copyright or explicitly waived

Key distinction: Copyright protects the expression. Patents protect the invention. Trade secrets protect confidential know-how. All three apply differently to the same AI system.

Case Study 1
Thaler v. Perlmutter (2023) — Can AI be an author?

Landmark US federal court ruling on AI authorship for copyright registration.

Case facts

  • Dr. Stephen Thaler created DABUS, an AI system that autonomously generated artwork titled "A Recent Entrance to Paradise"
  • Thaler applied to US Copyright Office to register the work listing DABUS as the sole author
  • Copyright Office refused — work lacked human authorship
  • Thaler sued Register of Copyrights Perlmutter in D.D.C.

Court US District Court, D.C. · Year 2023 · Outcome Affirmed USCO refusal

Court's holding & significance

  • "Human authorship is a bedrock requirement" of US copyright — rooted in Constitution's Copyright Clause
  • AI systems cannot be legal authors — no personhood, no legal standing
  • Human who prompts AI may have copyright in their selection and arrangement — but not the AI's raw output alone

Parallel: Thaler also lost patent cases (DABUS as inventor) — USPTO and Federal Circuit held inventors must be natural persons. Same principle across IP types.

Case Study 2
Getty Images v. Stability AI (2023–ongoing)

Major industry dispute — training generative AI on copyrighted images without license.

Dispute summary

  • Getty Images sued Stability AI in UK (2023) and US (SDNY, 2023)
  • Allegation: Stability AI scraped 12+ million Getty images from the web to train Stable Diffusion without permission or compensation
  • Getty claims: copyright infringement, trademark infringement (Getty watermark appeared in AI outputs), unfair competition
  • Stability AI defence: fair use (US) / text-and-data mining exception (UK)

Why this case matters for AI/ML

  • Tests whether training on copyrighted data = infringement or fair use
  • US fair use four-factor test: purpose, nature, amount, market effect — all contested for ML training
  • Precedent: Authors Guild v. Google (2015) — book scanning for search was fair use; but generative output competes with originals
  • Also relevant: NYT v. OpenAI (2023) — newspaper articles used to train ChatGPT

Status: Ongoing litigation — no final ruling yet. Outcome will shape the entire generative AI industry's data practices.

Supporting IP Types
Patent, trade secret & trademark in AI
⚙️

Patent

Definition: Exclusive right to an invention — novel, non-obvious, industrially applicable.

AI case: Thaler/DABUS — USPTO rejected AI as inventor (2022). Alice Corp v. CLS Bank (2014) — abstract software ideas not patentable without "inventive concept".

Example: Google's TPU chip patents; OpenAI's GPT architecture patent applications.

🔒

Trade Secret

Definition: Confidential information giving business advantage — protected if secrecy maintained.

AI case: OpenAI has NOT open-sourced GPT-4 weights — protected as trade secret. Google v. Uber (Waymo) — engineer stole self-driving trade secrets.

India: No standalone trade secret statute — protected under contract law & common law.

™

Trademark

Definition: Protects brand identifiers — prevents consumer confusion about product origin.

AI case: "ChatGPT" registered trademark (OpenAI). Getty watermark appearing in Stable Diffusion outputs = trademark + copyright claim.

India: Trade Marks Act 1999 — AI product names registerable in Class 9 (software) & Class 42 (SaaS).

Critical Analysis
Gaps, ethics & policy tensions

Gaps identified

  • Authorship vacuum — law requires human author; AI output falls into public domain by default
  • Training data rights — no clear global rule on scraping for ML; fair use is US-specific
  • Jurisdiction fragmentation — US, EU, UK, India all developing different rules simultaneously
  • Enforcement impracticality — millions of users generate infringing content daily
  • Open source vs closed — Meta LLaMA leak showed trade secret fragility

Ethical & innovation implications

  • Creator livelihoods — artists/writers whose work trains models receive no compensation (Getty argument)
  • Deepfakes & personality rights — copyright doesn't address non-consensual likeness use
  • Innovation access — strict copyright on training data could slow AI research (Stability AI argument)
  • Transparency — EU AI Act requires disclosure of training data sources for high-risk AI

My evaluation: Copyright alone cannot regulate AI — we need a layered framework combining copyright (outputs), trade secret (weights), patent (methods), and new sui generis rights for training data.

Q&A Preparation
Anticipated questions & how to answer

Likely questions

  • "Can I copyright ChatGPT's output?" → No, if purely AI-generated. Yes, for substantial human editing/selection (USCO 2023).
  • "Is training on web data legal?" → Unsettled. Fair use defence in US; opt-out system in EU; no clear rule in India.
  • "How is India different from the US?" → India lacks AI-specific copyright amendment; relies on §2(d) human author requirement.
  • "What about open-source AI models?" → License governs (Apache 2.0, MIT) — different from copyright registration.

Key arguments to defend

  • Copyright cannot be stretched to cover AI authorship without legislative change
  • Training data use is the most urgent unresolved issue — not output authorship
  • Layered IP approach (copyright + trade secret + patent) is more realistic than one-size-fits-all
  • Policy must balance creator rights vs innovation access — both are valid

Conclusion: AI hasn't broken IP law — it has exposed gaps that were always there. The next 2–3 years of court rulings (Getty, NYT) will define the industry.
